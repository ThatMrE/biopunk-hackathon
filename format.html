Two weekends,
bridged by a real lab week.

Most "biohackathons" are 48 hours of slideware. Biopunk's edge is the subsidized lab week in the middle — the part that turns a pitch into a living result. Three phases of escalating mutation.

Fri eve → Sun · 2 days

The petri dish. Form teams, ideate against the tracks, and pitch a one-week experiment.

  • Kickoff + team-formation mixer
  • Workshops: ideation, feasibility, biosafety, pitching
  • Sunday pitch to a panel — judges screen feasibility, novelty & safety
  • Green-lit pitches open lab access + in-kind reagents

Phase_01

The Prototype Weekend

Two days to turn a room of strangers into teams with a plan that's actually runnable at a BSL-1 bench in a week. Scope discipline here is the single biggest predictor of whether a team ships.

What happens

  • Friday: opening, tracks drop (e.g. Longevity × AI, Biosecurity, Wet-Lab Systems), team-formation mixer.
  • Saturday: ideation + feasibility workshops, mentor scoping, a "can you build this in a week?" gut-check with a lab lead.
  • Sunday: pitches to a panel, then feedback. Accepted teams get bench slots and reagent budgets.

Why the pitch is a gate

The pitch review is also the safety and feasibility gate. Judges and the lab's safety officer screen every proposal before it touches a bench — ruling out anything human-subject, clinical, animal, gain-of-function, or beyond BSL-1. A green-lit pitch is a promise the experiment is buildable, safe, and legal.

Lock this in on Sunday Custom DNA takes days to arrive. The moment a panel accepts a pitch, its synthesis order goes in — so reagents land on day one of the lab week instead of day four. This one decision is the difference between a real result and a failed build.
Phase_02

The Lab Week

The part nobody else does. Real bench access at a licensed BSL-1 lab — at Biopunk Lab HQ that's Frontier Tower's wet-lab floor, with $500k+ of gear — while sponsors cover the reagents that would otherwise make it impossible. A realistic one-cycle timeline:

Day 1 · Mon

Setup & prep

Pre-arrived synthetic DNA and the stock parts kit are waiting. Prep competent cells and media. Finalize the build plan with a mentor.

Day 2–3 · Tue–Wed

Build

Assembly (Golden Gate / Gibson / restriction–ligation), transformation, overnight growth, colony picks and minipreps.

Day 4 · Thu

Validate

Send constructs for ~$15 overnight whole-plasmid sequencing. Set up the functional assay.

Day 5 · Fri

Test & read out

Confirm sequences. Run the reporter, growth or biosensor assay. Collect the data that becomes the demo.

Weekend

Analyze & tell the story

Plot results, build the demo, frame what you learned and what's next.

✓

Buildable in a week

  • PCR, molecular cloning, bacterial/yeast transformation
  • Miniprep + overnight sequence validation
  • Small-scale reporter/protein expression & fluorescence assays
  • Simple biosensor prototypes; growth/inhibition assays
  • Basic CRISPR editing in bacteria or yeast
✕

Ruled out at the gate

  • Mammalian stable cell lines; animal or mouse work
  • Human subjects — including self-experimentation
  • Directed evolution / multi-week passaging
  • Large-scale fermentation; structural biology
  • Anything above BSL-1, clinical, or select-agent
The deliverable The demo-day output is a proof-of-concept plus a plan — "the construct expresses / the strain grows / the sensor lights up" — not a finished product. iGEM's Design–Build–Test–Learn cycle, compressed into one honest sprint.
Phase_03

Demo Day

The second weekend. Teams present living data to the tribunal — founders, investigators, and operators from the local bio scene. Run it science-fair style: teams at numbered stations, judges circulating, every project seen by at least three.

Rubric

How judges score

Rigor · did the data show what they claim?
Feasibility · is the next step real?
Novelty · is the idea fresh?
Responsibility · biosafety & societal thinking.
Story · can they make you care in 3 minutes?

Prizes

You get paid

Cash, in-kind bundles (sequencing runs, lab membership, glow plants, equipment time), follow-on micro-grants, and warm intros into the talent network. Value flows to the builder.

After

Kept forever

Every team writes its project up in the public gallery — a permanent portfolio piece with the data, the team, and the code. A résumé line that links to a real result.

The whole point In iGEM, a team pays ~€8,000+ and travels to a single Jamboree. In Biopunk, the same demo-day energy happens locally, repeatably, and the prize money moves toward the participants. Same talent-pipeline outcome, inverted economics.
